New Contextualism treats context not as a fixed backdrop but as ‘a living, layered, and evolving fabric’ [@reza-nitu-2025-people-place-justice]. Its dimensions overlap: a canal is at once an ecological system, a piece of urban infrastructure and an object of political contest; a pilgrimage site is simultaneously spiritual, cultural, social and economic.

Time is central to this view. The approach is described as ‘intrinsically forward-looking, yet profoundly anchored in an understanding of the past’ [@reza-2025-deltaic-bangladesh], and as a framework able to operate across time (past, present and future), scope (from ecology to culture) and scale (from building to city) [@reza-nitu-2025-people-place-justice]. ‘The primary focus in architecture and urban design must be designing for the future, not the present’, the founder writes, while acknowledging that ‘the future cannot be predicted completely’ [@reza-2023-new-contextualism-path].

Because conditions change, buildings and settlements are to be designed ‘not as static objects, but as adaptable systems’, able to accommodate future changes in use, technology and environment [@reza-2025-deltaic-bangladesh].

The published texts describe the dimensions as overlapping but do not specify how each relates to the others. The relationship map below and in the Framework section is therefore an editorial interpretation. It proposes the most direct relations between dimensions and, where possible, illustrates them with examples from the published record. It is offered for discussion, not as part of the founder’s framework.
