Contextualism has a long history in architectural thought. In its classic form, associated with the urban design teaching of Colin Rowe and the arguments of *Collage City* [@rowe-koetter-1978-collage-city], it read the city as a figure–ground field of solids and voids into which new buildings should fit. Esin Komez Daglioglu’s survey of the ‘context debate’ traces how the idea developed in architectural discourse from the 1960s and how it later fell out of favour [@komez-daglioglu-2015-context-debate].

New Contextualist writing acknowledges the value of that tradition but identifies its limit. Classic contextualism, Reza and Nitu argue, urged architects to echo the scale of neighbouring buildings, align rooflines and match materials, but ‘the movement often stopped at the surface’, leaving ‘deeper layers of culture, ecology, and history’ untouched [@reza-nitu-2025-people-place-justice].

The same critique is extended to the dominant paradigms of the twentieth century. Modernism, in the founder’s words, ‘often arrived in contexts like ours as an external imposition’, while Postmodernism ‘swung towards irony and surface quotation’ [@reza-2025-deltaic-bangladesh]. Placemaking ‘brought people back into the conversation’ but remained limited to small-scale interventions [@reza-nitu-2025-people-place-justice].

Against these positions, New Contextualism holds that context ‘transcends the immediate physical site’ [@reza-2025-deltaic-bangladesh]. It is not only what surrounds a building, but the social, economic, political, historical, ecological, scientific, geographical, cultural, urban and architectural conditions through which a place is made.
