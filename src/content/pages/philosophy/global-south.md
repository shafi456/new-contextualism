## Recognising Local Knowledge Systems

Local and vernacular building traditions represent accumulated knowledge developed through generations of interaction with specific environments.

These traditions often contain valuable lessons about climate responsiveness, material efficiency, spatial organisation, resource management, and community life.

Recognising this knowledge does not require reproducing historical architectural styles. Instead, it encourages architects to understand the principles behind established practices and reinterpret them through contemporary materials, technologies, and requirements.

For example, traditional transitional spaces, shaded verandas, courtyards, screens, and passive ventilation strategies can inform contemporary approaches to environmental comfort in hot and humid climates.

Similarly, traditional landscape and settlement practices can offer insights into water management, flood adaptation, and resource-sensitive development.

## Contextuality Across Different Societies

Although New Contextualism has developed from the particular realities of Bengal and the Global South, its fundamental approach is not geographically restricted.

Different societies possess distinct environmental conditions, cultural systems, historical experiences, and development challenges. A contextual approach allows these differences to become the foundation of design investigation.

Its wider relevance lies in the possibility of applying a consistent analytical approach while allowing the interpretation and outcomes to respond to individual places.

The philosophy therefore encourages dialogue between local knowledge and international architectural discourse, without treating either as universally sufficient.
