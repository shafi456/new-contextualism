## Understanding Context as Relational

New Contextualism understands context as a dynamic system of relationships rather than a collection of independent physical or social characteristics.

A place is shaped by the continuous interaction of its people, landscape, climate, economy, culture, governance, history, and built environment. These conditions influence one another and collectively determine how a place functions, develops, and acquires meaning.

For example, a settlement's geographical characteristics influence its ecological systems, patterns of occupation, economic activities, and infrastructure. These relationships subsequently affect its architectural forms, public spaces, and social organisation.

Understanding context therefore requires examining not only individual conditions but also the relationships through which they influence one another.



## Interdependence of Contextual Conditions

The contextual dimensions are interconnected and should not be investigated as isolated categories.

An ecological condition may influence local livelihoods and settlement patterns. Economic changes may transform land use and building practices. Political decisions may determine access to infrastructure and public resources. Cultural values may influence spatial organisation and the use of public and private environments.

These relationships are not necessarily equal or consistent across different places. Some contextual conditions may become more influential than others depending on the nature of the project.

Consequently, design decisions should emerge from an understanding of the most significant relationships and priorities within a particular context.

## Relationships Between People, Place and Time

The philosophy recognises three fundamental components of contextual understanding: people, place, and time.

**People** represent the communities, individuals, institutions, and social structures that experience and shape the built environment.

**Place** represents the physical, ecological, cultural, and spatial conditions within which human activities take place.

**Time** connects inherited conditions, contemporary realities, and anticipated transformations.

These components are continuously interrelated. People transform places through everyday activities and collective decisions, while places influence human behaviour, identity, and well-being. Time adds another dimension by introducing change, continuity, adaptation, and uncertainty.

Architecture becomes meaningful when it responds to these relationships and contributes positively to their future development.
