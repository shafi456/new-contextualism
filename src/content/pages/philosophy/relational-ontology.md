> [!NOTE] About the title of this page
> ‘Relational ontology’ is the term used in this platform’s structure. It does not appear in the published texts of New Contextualism, which describe context as ‘a complex, interwoven matrix of factors’ [@reza-2025-deltaic-bangladesh] and as ‘a living, layered, and evolving fabric’ [@reza-nitu-2025-people-place-justice]. This page sets out what those texts say. A statement by the founder on the term would be added here once published.

## Understanding Context as Relational

In New Contextualist writing, context is constituted by relations rather than by things. ‘Context, in the New Contextualist framework, transcends the immediate physical site. It encompasses a complex, interwoven matrix of factors’ [@reza-2025-deltaic-bangladesh]. The September 2025 essay states the point negatively: the approach ‘does not treat context as a backdrop or a single reference point, but as a living, layered, and evolving fabric’ [@reza-nitu-2025-people-place-justice].

What follows for design is a relation in both directions. The report of the first public workshop describes participants gaining ‘a heightened understanding of the interdependent connection between design and context’ [@bracu-2023-workshop-report], and the announcement of the 2025 symposium describes New Contextualism as ‘a way of seeing architecture as deeply rooted in the layered realities of place, people, and planet’ [@arch-bangla-2025-symposium-announcement].

## Interdependence of Contextual Conditions

No condition of a place is read alone. The September 2025 essay calls for ‘a holistic, integrative framework that recognizes that every site is at once ecological, social, cultural, political, economic, and temporal’ [@reza-nitu-2025-people-place-justice]. The founder traces this view to his early work in Barisal, where design meant ‘understanding settlement patterns shaped by water, livelihoods intertwined with the landscape, and social structures reflected in spatial arrangements’ [@reza-2025-deltaic-bangladesh].

The published case studies show the same interdependence. In a thesis on Dhaka’s Kallyanpur canal, ‘ecological, political, and urban layers carried greater weight’: the canal was framed ‘not as leftover infrastructure but as a civic spine, combining flood management with community access’ [@reza-nitu-2025-people-place-justice].

The texts describe the layers as overlapping, but they do not specify how each relates to the others. The network below, and the relations explored in the [Foundational Framework](/methodologies/foundational-framework/), are therefore an editorial interpretation, offered for discussion and not as part of the founder’s framework.

## Relationships Between People, Place and Time

Time is central to the approach. It is described as ‘intrinsically forward-looking, yet profoundly anchored in an understanding of the past’ [@reza-2025-deltaic-bangladesh], and as a framework able to operate across ‘time (past, present, future)’ [@reza-nitu-2025-people-place-justice]. ‘The primary focus in architecture and urban design must be designing for the future, not the present,’ the founder writes, acknowledging that ‘the future cannot be predicted completely’ [@reza-2023-new-contextualism-path].

People and place are joined through belonging. Buildings are to be ‘not isolated objects but catalysts for belonging, well-being, and resilience’, and the September 2025 essay ends with an image of the relation between context and design:

> ‘Think of context as melody, and design as harmony. Together, they can make spaces that honor memory, welcome life, and truly belong.’ [@reza-nitu-2025-people-place-justice]

Because people, places and their conditions change, buildings and settlements are to be designed ‘not as static objects, but as adaptable systems capable of accommodating future changes in use, technology, and environmental conditions’ [@reza-2025-deltaic-bangladesh].
