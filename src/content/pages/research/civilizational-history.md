New Contextualism grew in part from the founder’s doctoral research on the early Buddhist architecture of Bengal [@reza-2025-deltaic-bangladesh]. His recent essays address the cultural and civilisational history of Bengal, and a 2026 author note describes his work on ‘Bengal’s cultural history, deltaic urbanism, heritage governance, and civilisational continuity’ alongside ‘the new contextualism framework’ [@reza-2026-bongabdo].

> [!NOTE] About the names on this page
> The names Syncretic Cultural History Theory and Ganga-Brahmaputra Civilization, and their acronyms, are those of the platform’s structure. They have not been found in a published source. The sections below set out the published writing to which they correspond. The 2026 essays do not mention New Contextualism, and any connection between them and the approach is the editors’ reading.

## Syncretic Cultural History Theory (SCHT)

In an essay of June 2026, the founder argues that Bengal ‘is rarely permitted to speak in its own historical voice. Instead, it is repeatedly interpreted through civilisational frameworks produced elsewhere’ [@reza-2026-reading-bengal]. As an alternative, he proposes a method:

> ‘To understand Bengal and Islam historically, we need what might be called a syncretic cultural history. Not syncretism in the simplistic sense of religious mixing, but as a method of reading layered civilisational formation, a way of understanding how cultures interact, absorb, negotiate, adapt, and transform over time without losing their historical continuity.’ [@reza-2026-reading-bengal]

Such a history ‘does not deny religious difference, political conflict, or historical rupture. Rather, it recognises that societies evolve through interaction, adaptation, overlap, and continuity’ [@reza-2026-reading-bengal]. Its summary of Bengal’s history is one of localisation: ‘Islam entered Bengal, but Bengal reshaped Islam. Sanskritic traditions entered Bengal, but Bengal vernacularised them’ [@reza-2026-reading-bengal].

The idea of a culture formed through layered encounters is also present in the founder’s writing on New Contextualism, which describes ‘the process that shaped Baņgālee culture itself through encounters with Vedic, Buddhist, Jain, Islamic, and European influences’ [@reza-2025-deltaic-bangladesh], and in a 2021 article on the cultural continuity of Sultanate Bengal [@reza-2021-sultanate-ponds].

## Ganga-Brahmaputra Civilization (GBC)

The same essay traces Bengal’s formation to ‘a civilisational logic of its own: rooted in the Ganges-Brahmaputra delta, in an earth-centric cosmology that venerated water, fertility, the feminine divine, and the cyclical rhythms of a great alluvial world’ [@reza-2026-reading-bengal]. ‘The Bengal delta, at the convergence of the Ganges and the Brahmaputra, nurtured a civilisation of extraordinary antiquity and sophistication’, one in which Bengal ‘was never a periphery but a civilisational centre in its own right’ [@reza-2026-reading-bengal].

An essay of May 2026 applies the idea to the Bengali calendar. Bongabdo, the founder argues, is ‘a long-term expression of Bengal’s delta civilisation, later changed and adapted by different rulers’; it ‘emerged from the deeper continuity of a deltaic civilisation that learned to organise life around rivers, monsoons, cultivation, ritual, and renewal long before empires attempted to regulate it’ [@reza-2026-bongabdo].

A 2023 article in Bengali on the origins of the Natha tradition likewise places its subject in the Ganga–Brahmaputra region of Bengal [@reza-2023-natha-bengal].
