Heritage has been part of New Contextualism from its origins in the founder’s research on the early architecture of Bengal. In the New Contextualist perspective, heritage ‘is a vital, living continuum—a repository of knowledge, identity, and meaning that must actively inform contemporary design’ [@reza-2025-deltaic-bangladesh].

## New Contextual Heritage Management (NCHM)

The name appears in the title of a 2026 article by Reza and Rahman on the Buddhist monastery of Somapura Mahavihara, ‘A New Contextual Heritage Management of a Buddhist Sacred Land’ [@reza-rahman-2026-somapura]. Its abstract proposes ‘a New Contextualist framework to restore its spiritual aura and amplify its global appeal’, drawing on lessons from Buddhist pilgrimage centres including Bodh Gaya, Lumbini and Bagan [@reza-rahman-2026-somapura].

The year before, the founder was listed to present New Contextualism at the State Heritage Conference 2025 in Western Australia. The abstract of that presentation argues that it ‘offers a valuable framework for creating sustainable, equitable, and culturally sensitive heritage sites’, and proposes to ‘discuss how these principles can be applied to heritage sites, using case studies to illustrate the potential of this approach’ [@wa-heritage-2025-programme].

## Cultural Continuity and Change

The texts reject both the preservation of heritage apart from life and its replication. Heritage is ‘not a static relic to be cordoned off or merely replicated’; understanding its layers, narratives and embedded wisdom ‘allows us to create interventions that are respectful yet innovative, fostering a sense of continuity rather than rupture’ [@reza-2025-deltaic-bangladesh]. The aim is ‘building upon legacy, not just beside it’ [@reza-2025-deltaic-bangladesh].

The founder’s historical research supplies examples of continuity through change. A 2021 article reads the pond beside almost every Sultanate mosque of Bengal as a sign of the continuity of Bengali tradition, within a culture ‘evolved through a long process of hybridization’ [@reza-2021-sultanate-ponds].

## Tangible and Intangible Heritage

The 2026 article argues for ‘an integrated management strategy that harmonizes conservation of tangible and intangible heritage, community participation, and sustainable tourism innovation’, with ‘interpretive interventions, revived rituals, and inclusive stakeholder engagement’ [@reza-rahman-2026-somapura].

The documented proposals treat intangible heritage as a design generator. The Panatirtha thesis, at a pilgrimage site traditionally visited once a year, drew on ‘spiritual & cultural concepts, especially Advaita, or Non-dualism’ to shape its spatial form [@reza-nitu-2025-people-place-justice]. The Alpana Gram proposal concerns a rural settlement ‘celebrated for its vibrant Alpana art’ [@reza-2025-deltaic-bangladesh].

## Heritage Values and Stakeholders

Communities are presented as participants in managing heritage, and as its beneficiaries. The Alpana Gram proposal aimed to make the village ‘a community-based tourism destination to manage tourism's impact on the existing settlement fabric and benefit the community’ [@reza-2025-deltaic-bangladesh]. The founder’s earlier work includes a community-involved strategic management plan for Chhoto Katra in Old Dhaka [@reza-2018-chhoto-katra]. A 2024 thesis that took its inspiration from New Contextualism challenged a planned five-star hotel that, in its account, ‘would have uprooted the local Murong tribe’ [@arch-bangla-2024-mru-settlement].
